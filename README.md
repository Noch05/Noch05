### Method Development & Software

- **Adaptive Experimental Designs**: using the `{whatifbandit}` package below to systematically evaluate response-adaptive experiments compared to Randomized Controlled Trials in public policy experimental settings. My goal is to promote adaptive designs more broadly and to provide researchers with the necessary tools to decide on a design based on their experiment's unique circumstances. Response-adaptive designs have more optimal allocation patterns, increasing general welfare from field experiments, without completely compromising the ability to identify causal effects, but they come with important estimator variance trade-offs. Here I dove deep into the multi-armed bandit and causal inference literature, formalizing my understanding of both fields. 

- **`{whatifbandit}`**: an open-source R package I built for simulating response-adaptive trials using multi-armed bandits. It supports pure simulations from scratch and resimulations of RCTs under adaptive conditions. Currently supports experiments with binary outcomes, Thompson Sampling and UCB1 bandit decision algorithms, and a host of other customizations to tailor the simulation appropriately. Currently available on [CRAN](https://cran.r-project.org/package=whatifbandit). Development version on [GitHub](https://github.com/Noch05/whatifbandit). **Presented at PolMeth 2026**. Planned extensions into more outcome types, contextual bandits, and more advanced simulation settings. Here I learned the basics of software development, such as API design, detailed documentation, and the important speed and memory usage tradeoffs in designing each function.
 
### Applied Research Projects

- **Political Donor Classification**: Using several years of survey data from CES and elementary machine learning methods (logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosted trees) to examine the behavior of political donors. Assessing predictive performance using F1, accuracy, precision, and recall, and making substantive inferences on donor behavior using variable importance measures. Standard methods in machine learning are used, such as the train-test split and cross-validation, to obtain accurate estimates of model performance and calibrate model hyperparameters. **Presented at SPSA 2026**. [Code, data, and paper](https://github.com/Noch05/predict_donor_behavior). Here I learned the basic R and Python tools for machine learning, and how to change my traditional inference-focused mindset to draw substantive insights out of prediction tasks.

- **Regional Autonomy and Economic Growth**: panel-data case study of northern Italian regions and German Länder, estimating the impact of regional autonomy on GDP and economic growth using linear fixed effects models. [Code, data, and paper](https://github.com/Noch05/it_de_regional_autonomy_analysis). Here I expanded my knowledge of panel-data methods, rigorously understanding the assumptions and use cases of fixed effects, random effects, and mixed-level effects models, ultimately deciding to keep things simple with fixed effects. I also had to utilize my macroeconomic knowledge to properly compare the regions over time and across countries since inflation (obviously) inflates real growth rates, and Germany and Italy had different currencies at the time. Luckily, there was an existing dataset of real GDP measured in PPP to use, but I briefly toyed with the concept of computing it myself, from the available nominal values, using the techniques from my macroeconomics courses.

- **City Managers and Voter Turnout**: Standard multiple regression analysis to estimate the impact of city manager government systems on voter turnout in municipal elections, combined with propensity score matching techniques. Municipalities with city managers typically have much weaker elected officials, because power is concentrated in the manager's position. In these cities, elected officials establish broad policy directives, while the manager implements them, as opposed to cities without managers, where elected officials are directly responsible for designing broad policies, their detailed implementations, and administering them properly, thus having more power. A rational theory of voting suggests, therefore, that cities without managers would have higher turnout, because voters' choice of elected officials would impact the policies and day-to-day operations of the municipal government more than in a city with a manager. This paper empirically tests, using hitherto unused municipal electoral data and self-collected city manager data, whether this is the case. The [paper](https://edspace.american.edu/clocksandclouds/wp-content/uploads/sites/115/2025/04/Spring-2025-Final-Publication-Digital.pdf) was **published in American University's undergraduate journal, Clocks and Clouds, in Spring 2025**. [Code and data](https://github.com/Noch05/city_managers). This was my first project; I learned the basics of multiple regression analysis and how to conduct reproducible research using R, and the R Markdown and Quarto technical writing tools. I played around with the concept of propensity score matching, at the recommendation of my professor, to draw some more causal inferences from the observational data, but now I realize I should have kept it simpler since the method does not magically induce causality.
